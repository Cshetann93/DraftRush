The Complete Technical Bible

Screenplay Format Mastery: Every Element Inside a Script Explained

A screenplay is an industrial document designed to convert typography into footage. Every indent, tab stop, capitalization rule, and parenthetical exists to communicate specific logistical commands to the director, assistant director, editor, and actors.

By Lead Writer: CHETAN • UPDATED FOR 2026 • 14 MIN READ
TL;DR

The Golden Rules of Screenplay Layout

Never deviate from these five baseline specifications:

  • Font: 12-point Courier (or Courier Prime). Non-proportional monospace only.
  • Page Size: US Letter (8.5 x 11 inches). Never A4 for Hollywood spec submissions.
  • Margins: Left: 1.5 inches (binding space); Right: 1.0 inch; Top/Bottom: 1.0 inch.
  • Pacing Ratio: 1 page of clean format = 1 minute of screen time.
  • Tense: Present tense only (third person). If a camera cannot film it, it does not belong on the page.

The Complete Encyclopedia of 16 Script Elements

ELEMENT 01 Scene Heading (Master Slugline)

The anchor of screenplay logistics. It informs the assistant director and crew whether they are filming inside or outside, the physical set, and the lighting requirement.

• Camera Environment: INT. (Interior) or EXT. (Exterior). For scenes spanning both: INT./EXT. CAR - MOVING - DAY.
• Location: Specific physical setting (e.g. METRO TERMINAL - VAULT 4).
• Time of Day: Standard values are DAY or NIGHT. Avoid ambiguous creative times like "DUSK" or "TWILIGHT" unless lighting is critical.
ELEMENT 02 Action Block (Scene Description)

Describes what the camera photographs and what the microphone captures. Always present tense, third person.

Rule: Keep paragraphs to a maximum of 3 to 4 lines. One paragraph break signals a shift in camera perspective or eye-trace, preserving page white space.
ELEMENT 03 Character Introduction

The very first time a character appears in action text, their name must be CAPITALIZED, followed by their age and a succinct, filmable physical or behavioral description.

Correct: MARCUS VANCE (40s), disheveled, nursing a swollen jaw and clutching an uncashed paycheck.
ELEMENT 04 Character Cue (Dialogue Header)

Positioned 3.7 inches from the left margin, fully capitalized. Indicates who is speaking. Consistency is required: never change a character from "DETECTIVE VANCE" to "MARCUS" midway through the draft.

ELEMENT 05 Character Extensions: (V.O.) vs (O.S.)

Placed right beside the character name in parentheses:

  • • (O.S.) Off-Screen: The character is physically inside the location of the scene, but outside the camera's frame (e.g. shouting from the hallway). In television multicam formats, this is written as (O.C.) for Off-Camera.
  • • (V.O.) Voice-Over: The character is not physically present in the scene (e.g. narration, telephone voice, podcast audio, or internal thoughts).
ELEMENT 06 Parentheticals (Wrylies)

A short instruction between the character name and spoken dialogue. Placed at 3.1 inches.

Rule: Use only to clarify subtext that contradicts the literal words (e.g. (sarcastic) or (whispering)). Never use parentheticals for extensive physical actions.
ELEMENT 07 Dialogue Block

Indented 2.5 inches from the left edge, constrained to a maximum width of 3.5 to 3.8 inches. Dialogue should never stretch across the full width of the page.

ELEMENT 08 Dual Dialogue

Used when two characters speak at the exact same time, interrupting or talking over one another. Placed as two parallel columns of dialogue side-by-side on the page.

ELEMENT 09 Sub-Headers (Internal Sluglines)

Used to move between specific areas within a larger master location without writing a full scene heading every time.

Example: If master is INT. POLICE HEADQUARTERS - DAY, sub-headers can simply be: CAPTAIN'S OFFICE or EVIDENCE LOCKER.
ELEMENT 10 Chyrons, Titles, and Superimpositions

Used when text, timestamps, or location titles must be superimposed on the video screen.

Format: TITLE ON SCREEN: "THREE YEARS LATER" or SUPER: "TOKYO INTERNATIONAL AIRPORT".
ELEMENT 11 Intercuts (Cross-Cutting)

Eliminates the need to write back-and-forth sluglines every three seconds during telephone calls or simultaneous cross-cutting action.

Format: INTERCUT MARCUS'S APARTMENT / SARAH'S VEHICLE:
Once declared, write dialogue continuously without new headings.
ELEMENT 12 Montage & Series of Shots

Used to condense time through a sequence of rapid, connected shots.

SERIES OF SHOTS - THE CRIME WAVE:
A) Storefront window smashes into shards.
B) Cash registers emptied into duffel bags.
C) Police cruisers stranded in gridlock traffic.
ELEMENT 13 Capitalized Sound Effects (SFX)

Important physical sounds in action paragraphs are CAPITALIZED so sound designers and foley artists can log them during breakdown passes.

Example: The deadbolt CLICKS shut. An engine ROARS to life.
ELEMENT 14 Flashbacks & Dream Sequences

Must be clearly tagged in the scene heading to prevent reader confusion. Always remember to declare when the script returns to the primary timeline.

Start: FLASHBACK: EXT. CAMPGROUND - 10 YEARS EARLIER - NIGHT
End: BACK TO PRESENT:
ELEMENT 15 Transitions

Editing cues right-aligned at 6.0 inches. Common types: CUT TO:, MATCH CUT TO:, SMASH CUT TO:, DISSOLVE TO:.

Rule: Use sparingly. Modern scripts assume cuts between scenes; only add a transition if it conveys a dramatic jolt or thematic contrast.
ELEMENT 16 Page Numbers & Scene Numbers

Page numbers appear in the top-right corner followed by a period (e.g. 24.), flush with the 1.0-inch top margin. The title page is never numbered, and page one is never numbered.

Spec Rule: Never include scene numbers on a spec script. Scene numbers are added only on shooting drafts by production staff during scheduling.

The 5-Minute Format Pre-Flight Checklist

Before exporting your final PDF, run these checks to ensure you meet professional studio standards:

CHECK 01
Page Count Range: Feature spec scripts must sit between 90 and 115 pages. TV drama pilots: 50–60 pages. 30-minute comedy pilots: 28–34 pages. Anything over 120 pages is an instant pass for most readers.
CHECK 02
Orphaned Lines: Ensure no character names sit alone at the very bottom of a page with dialogue starting on the next page.
CHECK 03
The Parenthetical Purge: Remove unnecessary emotion wrylies. If the line conveys anger, remove (angry) and let the dialogue speak for itself.
