Definitive Formatting Manual

Screenplay Format Mastery: The Complete Spec Script Guide

Screenplay formatting is not creative expression. It is a strict technical blueprint designed to equate one page of text to roughly one minute of screen time. Master the rules, margin positions, and element hierarchies required by Hollywood studio readers.

BY DRAFTSRUSH STUDIO • UPDATED FOR 2026 • 11 MIN READ
TL;DR

Screenplay Formatting in 60 Seconds

Strict formatting rules ensure that production teams can estimate timing, budget, and department requirements:

  • Typography: Exclusively 12-point Courier (or Courier Prime), 10 characters per inch, single-spaced.
  • Page Timing Ratio: 1 page of properly formatted script equals approximately 1 minute of screen time.
  • Margins: 1.5-inch left margin (for three-hole punch binding); 1.0-inch right, top, and bottom margins.
  • Core Elements: Scene Heading (Slugline), Action Block, Character Name, Parenthetical, Dialogue, Extension, and Transition.

Technical Rules

Standard Screenplay Margin Geometry

COURIER 12PT SPECS

Screenplay software handles these margins automatically. If you ever need to calibrate custom templates in Google Docs or Word, these exact tab stop positions from the left edge of the page are mandatory:

Page Size & Margins: 8.5" x 11" (US Letter). Left: 1.5", Right: 1.0", Top/Bottom: 1.0"
Scene Heading / Action: Starts at 1.5" from left edge (Full width across page to 7.5")
Character Cue: Starts at 3.7" from left edge (Capitalized)
Parenthetical: Starts at 3.1" from left edge (Max width 3.0")
Dialogue: Starts at 2.5" from left edge (Max width 3.5" to 3.8")
Transitions: Starts at 6.0" from left edge (Right aligned)

The 7 Building Blocks of Screenplay Format

Every line in your draft belongs to one of these seven distinct formatting categories:

01 Scene Heading (Slugline)

Tells the crew where and when the camera is filming. Always capitalized. Format: [INT. or EXT.] [LOCATION] - [TIME OF DAY].

Example: EXT. HARBOR WAREHOUSE - DOCK 4 - NIGHT
02 Action Paragraphs

Describes what the audience sees and hears. Written strictly in present tense. Cap paragraphs at 3–4 lines to preserve white space and reading speed.

Rule: Never describe thoughts or emotions that cannot be filmed.
03 Character Name Introductions

The first time a character appears in an action block, their name must be CAPITALIZED with an age and short visual trait. Above dialogue blocks, the name is centered and capitalized.

Example: SARAH CHEN (30s), sharp-eyed with ink-stained fingers.
04 Character Extensions: (V.O.) vs (O.S.)

Extensions appear immediately beside the character's name in parentheses:

  • • (O.S.) / Off-Screen: Character is physically in the scene space, but not framed by the camera (e.g. shouting from an adjoining room).
  • • (V.O.) / Voice-Over: Character is not physically in the scene (e.g. narrator, phone voice, internal thoughts).
05 Parentheticals (Wrylies)

Short directions placed between the character name and spoken dialogue. Use sparingly; only when a line delivery would be completely misinterpreted without it (e.g. sarcasm or whispering).

Example: (sotto voce) or (whispering into the phone)
06 Dialogue Block

The spoken text. Constrained to approximately 3.5 inches wide, centered beneath the character's name. Never justify dialogue text; leave the right margin ragged.

07 Transitions

Editing cues like CUT TO:, DISSOLVE TO:, or SMASH CUT TO: right-aligned at the end of a scene. In modern spec scripts, standard cuts are assumed; only use transitions when a dramatic shift in tone occurs.

Title Page Formatting Guidelines

The title page is the first thing a coverage reader evaluates. It should be minimalist and devoid of amateur clutter:

1. Center of Page: Title in ALL CAPS, Courier 12pt. Followed two lines down by "written by", followed two lines down by your name.
2. Bottom Left Corner: Contact information (Name, Email Address, Phone Number).
3. Bottom Right Corner: WGA registration number or copyright notice (optional for spec drafts).
What to NEVER Include: Draft numbers, dates, loglines, budget notes, cast wishlists, or promotional artwork. These signal an amateur submission immediately.
