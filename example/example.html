The Reality Check

Why 90% of short films get rejected.

Most short films fail before production ever begins.

Writers treat short films like miniature feature films. Festivals, producers, and audiences do not want compressed epics; they want precise, singular friction.

RULE 01: SCOPE
02/12
Core Concept

Find a Small World

One Arena, Zero Sprawl

A short film cannot support five subplots, ten locations, or four flashbacks.

Pick one contained arena: a late-night diner, an interrogation desk, or an elevator between floors. Spatial restraint forces you to focus entirely on human tension.

RULE 02: PACING
03/12
Scene Timing

Enter Late, Leave Early

Cut The Warm-Up

Never show characters waking up, parking cars, or exchanging greetings.

Open the scene at the final second before a decision happens. Cut away the instant the consequence lands. Every page must fight for its screen time.

RULE 03: MOTIVATION
04/12
Character Goal

Isolate a Single Want

One Objective

Your protagonist needs one concrete target within the first 60 seconds.

Not "finding inner happiness," but unlocking a safe, reaching a phone before it dies, or hiding evidence. Make the physical objective unmistakable.

RULE 04: PRACTICALITY
05/12
Production Reality

Write for Real Assets

Audit Before Writing

Do not write exploding warehouses or police chases on an indie budget.

Catalog what you already control: a friend’s tool shed, a basement storage room, or two talented actors. Build a premise that turns your actual constraints into visual style.

RULE 05: SHOW VS TELL
06/12
Cinematic Weight

Cut 50% of the Dialogue

Actions Over Speeches

Beginner scripts explain emotion through conversation.

Cinema communicates through physical behavior: nervous hands, locking a deadbolt, or refusing to make eye contact. If an action conveys the truth, delete the line.

RULE 06: RUNTIME
07/12
The Programmer's Rule

Hit the 10-Minute Sweet Spot

Festival Math

A 25-minute short kills festival chances. Programmers can screen two 10-minute shorts in the same programming block.

Aim for 8 to 12 pages in standard format. Tight pacing keeps submission acceptance rates high and viewer drop-off low.

RULE 07: RETENTION
08/12
Tension Maintenance

Turn the Screws Often

No Mid-Script Slumps

Every 90 seconds, alter the balance of power.

Whenever characters make progress, introduce a fresh consequence or reveal counter-evidence. Sustained tension prevents audience attention from flatlining.

RULE 08: THE CLIMAX
09/12
The Final Beat

Avoid Gimmick Endings

Inevitability

"It was all a dream" or sudden unearned deaths leave audiences feeling cheated.

Your climax should feel surprising yet inevitable. The resolution must come directly from your character's choices, not convenient coincidence.

RULE 09: INDUSTRY STANDARD
10/12
Presentation

Respect Industry Formats

Courier 12pt Standard

Readers judge scripts within the first 30 seconds of reading.

Use standard screenplay margins, avoid directing the camera in action blocks, and keep action descriptions to two-line clusters. Clean pages get read first.

RULE 10: EXECUTION
11/12
The Final Step

Ship the First Draft

Writing Is Rewriting

You cannot fix an empty screen.

Write your rough draft from page one to the end without stopping to polish. Give it a cold table read, eliminate passive exposition, and lock your shooting script.
